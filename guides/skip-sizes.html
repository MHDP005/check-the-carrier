Size guide

Skip sizes explained: 4 to 12 yard skips and skip bags

Skip sizes are sold in cubic yards, but what matters is weight as well as space. Heavy rubble and soil fill a skip’s weight limit long before it looks full. Here is what the common sizes measure, what local licensed firms publish, and the rules for a skip on the road.

Figures on this page are what each firm published on its own website when we checked on 21 November 2026 (Europe/London). Sizes, dimensions and “from” prices vary between companies and change over time. They are not quotes. Always get a price for your own postcode and confirm what the firm will and will not take.

What “yard” means

A “6-yard skip” holds roughly 6 cubic yards. One cubic yard is about 0.76 cubic metres, so a 6-yard skip is a little under 4.6 m³ and a 12-yard skip is about 9.2 m³ (our own conversion). Skips taper towards the base, and each firm builds to slightly different dimensions, so treat any size as approximate.

Firms usually describe capacity in bin bags. Those counts are rough marketing estimates, but they are a useful way to compare one company with another.

Why heavy waste needs a smaller skip

All three firms say the same thing in different words: soil, hardcore, brick and concrete belong in a smaller skip. FJL explains that heavy materials “reach the weight limit before the skip is full”. In practice that means:

  • Garden soil, patio slabs, a knocked-through wall: ask for a 4, 6 or 8-yard skip, and tell the firm what the waste is when you book.
  • A house or loft clearance with furniture and boxes: a 10 or 12-yard skip may be better value, as long as it really is light waste.
  • A kitchen or bathroom refit: mixed waste, so an 8-yard builder’s skip is the size most firms point to. Ask about any heavy items, such as tiles or a cast-iron bath.
  • Mixed heavy and bulky waste: if you hire one big skip, check the firm’s weight terms first. If you are unsure, ask whether two smaller collections, or a separate grab or muck-away service, would be cheaper.

Fill to the top, not above it

Allwaste’s hire guide says a skip “must not be loaded to a level higher than the top”. Most firms have a similar rule because an overloaded skip may not be lifted safely or legally. If the waste is piled above the sides, the driver may leave some behind or charge more. If you think you are close, hire the next size up.

Some items cannot go in an ordinary skip, whatever the size. Allwaste lists asbestos, fridges, freezers, batteries and tyres as excluded. FJL lists asbestos, paint, solvents, oils and other liquids, batteries, electricals and vapes, fluorescent tubes, gas bottles and tyres as needing separate disposal, and asks you to keep plasterboard separate from mixed waste and mention it when you book. Ask before you load anything unusual.

Will it fit, and can it go on the road?

Measure your driveway before you choose. Using Langley Skip Hire’s published sizes, a 6-yard skip needs a space of about 2.4 m × 1.5 m, and a 12-yard skip about 4 m × 1.8 m, plus room for the lorry to reach it. Check overhead cables, branches and gates as well.

If the skip has to go on the road, pavement or verge, the law sets the rules:

  • Permission is required. Under section 139 of the Highways Act 1980, a builder’s skip must not be put on a highway without the highway authority’s permission. The council can attach conditions, including about the skip’s dimensions, siting, lighting and removal.
  • Councils do set size limits. For example, the Royal Borough of Windsor and Maidenhead says a licensed container must not exceed 5 m in length and 2 m in width. Applications must be made at least 10 working days ahead, and only by skip companies registered with the council. Fees as at 1 April 2026 start at £90 for the admin fee plus the first week.
  • In Slough, Slough Borough Council issues highway skip permits only to registered skip-hire companies, for 14 days with 7-day renewals. The council says it is the hirer’s responsibility to make sure the skip is authorised.
  • The skip owner must make sure a skip on the highway is properly lit in the dark, marked with the owner’s name and phone number or address, and removed as soon as practicable once full (section 139(4)). Skips on the carriageway must also carry the reflective end markings set out in the Builders’ Skips (Markings) Regulations 1984.

Some firms, such as FJL, say they place skips only on private land. Others, such as Allwaste and HIPPO, say they arrange permits. Ask before you book if your skip cannot go on a driveway.

A quick way to choose

  1. Describe the waste honestly: heavy (soil, rubble, concrete), light (furniture, packaging, timber) or mixed.
  2. Heavy waste: 4 to 8 yards. Light, bulky waste: 10 to 12 yards or more. A small job: consider a skip bag or the council’s bulky waste collection.
  3. Measure the space, and decide whether the skip will be on private land or the highway.
  4. Get two or three prices for your postcode, and compare the total including VAT, the hire period, weight limits and any permit fee.
  5. Check the carrier on the Environment Agency register before anything is collected, and keep the receipt. Our licence-checking guide shows how.
